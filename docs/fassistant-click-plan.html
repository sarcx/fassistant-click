Building fassistant-click

An Android auto-clicker with Click Assistant's feature set, written from scratch in the conventions of its sibling repo. Nine milestones, riskiest platform unknown first.

What. A sideloaded Android app that places tap points on the screen and drives them itself: taps, straight and curved swipes, pinch and spread, multi‑finger versions of all of them, with per‑point delay, hold time and repeat count, saved as named scripts. The floating panel stays on top of whatever app you are using. No root.

How. One mechanism does all of it — AccessibilityService.dispatchGesture(). Every feature in the list above is a different shape of GestureDescription handed to that one call. There is no second path to synthetic touch without root, so the stack question answers itself: Kotlin, no runtime dependencies, hand‑rolled views — the same build as fassistant-android, in its own repo with its own app id, signing key and self‑update URL.

The hard part is not the gestures, it is being allowed to send them. Three permission gates stand in front of the app, and on Android 13 and later the accessibility gate is deliberately hardened against exactly this kind of sideloaded app. Two questions about that could not be settled from documentation, so milestone 0 is a throwaway probe module that answers them on the real phones before any real code is written — the same move fassistant-android made with its own probe/.

One call does everything

Worth getting the shape of it before anything else, because it explains why the feature list is cheaper than it looks and why the app has an awkward relationship with its own floating panel.

The second consequence of the diagram is the good news. Because every gesture is the same GestureDescription handed to the same call, the difference between a tap, a 500 ms swipe, a two-finger pinch and a curved drag is entirely in how the strokes are laid out in time and space. That is one builder, not five features.

Those two ceilings — how many strokes one gesture may hold and how long it may last — are read at runtime from GestureDescription.getMaxStrokeCount() and getMaxGestureDuration() rather than hardcoded, matching the sibling repo's habit of deciding platform facts on the phone. A run longer than the duration ceiling is split into consecutive gestures; a gesture wanting more fingers than the phone allows is refused with a message rather than silently truncated.

Continued gestures need Android 8.0. Holding a finger down across more than one Path — an L‑shaped drag, a swipe that pauses mid‑stroke — uses the willContinue flag on StrokeDescription and continueStroke(), both API 26. Below that the app offers single-path gestures only. verified in the API docs

Three gates before the first tap

None of the code matters until the phone lets the service run. Each gate fails differently and each has a different route through it, and the third one is where Android has deliberately made life hard for a sideloaded app that asks for accessibility.

One thing the gate diagram cannot settle: whether an accessibility service declared by an app targeting SDK 25 is even offered in the Accessibility settings list on a modern phone. Nothing in the documentation says the enumeration filters on target level, and the adb route writes the setting without consulting that list at all — but the sibling repo learned the hard way that a low targetSdk buys less than the docs imply, so this goes to the probe rather than into an assumption.

Stack

There is not much of a decision here, which is worth saying plainly rather than dressing up as a trade-off study. Synthetic touch on an unrooted Android phone has exactly one public API, that API is only reachable from an AccessibilityService, and an accessibility service is Kotlin or Java. Everything else in the table is a way of not building the app.

Kotlin + dispatchGesture Every gesture in the feature list, on any unrooted phone from Android 7.0. Reads the same as fassistant-android, so both repos are one codebase to hold in your head. The accessibility grant, and the Android 13+ dance around it. pick

The one feature that might not be buildable

The original records a sequence of gestures you perform and plays it back. Recording coordinates is easy — a full-screen capture window takes your taps and writes them down. Recording a live interaction with another app is a different thing, because it needs the touch to reach that app and to reach us, at the same time.

An accessibility service is given the semantic tree of the screen, not the raw touch stream. I could not find a documented, non-root way for one to observe another app's touch coordinates while those touches still land normally. That may exist and I have not found it, or it may not exist; either way it is not something to guess at in a plan, so it is a probe question with a fallback already designed.

ShapeHow it worksWhat you give upStatus
Stepped capture A full-screen transparent window takes your taps. Each one becomes a point, and the real gap between them becomes that point's delay. You tap out the sequence, then close the window and run it. The app underneath does not react while you are recording, so you are tapping against a static screen rather than following a live flow. fallback, build this
Live observation Watch real touches as they pass through to the target app, and record them without swallowing them. Nothing, if it turns out to be possible. M0 question 4
Stepped capture covers the common case — replaying a fixed sequence on a screen whose layout does not move — which is most of what recording is used for.

Milestones

Ordered so the thing most likely to invalidate the plan is settled first, and so every milestone after that is a slice you can actually run. Nothing here builds UI for a capability that has not already been proven on a phone.

0

The probe answers 4 unknowns

A throwaway second module, exactly like the sibling's probe/: a single screen that runs the four checks the documentation would not settle and prints what it found. Install it on the old OnePlus and on a phone running Android 13 or later, because the interesting answers differ between them.

  1. Is a targetSdk 25 accessibility service listed and enableable in Accessibility settings on a modern phone?
  2. Does writing enabled_accessibility_services over adb turn it on without the restricted-settings dialog?
  3. What do getMaxStrokeCount() and getMaxGestureDuration() actually return here?
  4. Does a dispatched tap land on our own touchable overlay, and does FLAG_NOT_TOUCHABLE hand it through to the app underneath? Is there any way to observe a real touch without swallowing it?
How you knowFour answers on screen, written into CHANGELOG.md the way the sibling recorded its own findings. If question 1 comes back no, targetSdk goes up before a line of the app is written — which is the entire point of doing this first.
1

One hardcoded tap

The thinnest possible end-to-end slice: an accessibility service with canPerformGestures, and one hardcoded tap at the middle of the screen fired on a five-second timer. No panel, no markers, no settings, no UI at all.

How you knowOpen the calculator, leave the phone alone, and watch a digit press itself. adb logcat -s fclick shows the GestureResultCallback reporting completion rather than cancellation — worth distinguishing, because a cancelled gesture is what a blocked dispatch looks like.
2

Panel and markers the overlay trap

The floating panel and one draggable marker, both overlay windows. The panel is never focusable, so the app underneath keeps input focus. The marker is touchable while you drag it and non-touchable for the length of a run. Start and stop buttons on the panel.

How you knowDrag the marker over a link in Chrome and hit start — the link opens. adb shell dumpsys window | grep fassistantclick shows the marker's flags flipping when the run begins and back when it ends.
3

The timing engine

Delay before, hold duration, repeat count, and an infinite mode. A countdown before the first gesture. Pause, resume and stop mid-run. A foreground service owns the run so it is not killed halfway, and its notification is the way to stop a run when the panel is in the way.

How you knowPoint it at a tally-counter app, ask for 100 repeats at 200 ms, and read 100 off the screen. Logcat timestamps show the actual interval, which is what tells you whether the gap needs to be scheduled rather than slept.
4

Every gesture kind

Swipe, curved swipe, pinch, spread, and multi-finger versions of all of them, plus several points in one script. The builder reads the two runtime ceilings and splits or refuses accordingly. Continued strokes on API 26 and up; single-path only below.

How you knowA pinch zooms Google Maps out. A curved swipe draws a visible curve in a sketch app. Asking for more fingers than the phone allows produces a message naming the limit, not a crash and not a truncated gesture.
5

Saved scripts

Name a script, save it, list them, load one, delete one. Plain files in the app's own storage — no database, matching the sibling's approach to its watchlist.

How you knowSave a five-point script, force-stop the app with adb shell am force-stop, reopen, load it, run it, get the same result.
6

Recording

Whichever shape milestone 0 says is possible. Stepped capture unless it found a way to watch touches pass through.

How you knowRecord five taps across the calculator keypad, replay, and get the same five digits in the same order with the same rhythm.
7

Setup and polish

Panel size and opacity, because it sits on top of the app you are trying to use. A first-run screen showing the three gates with a live open-or-closed state and a deep link to each — the sibling already has this pattern in its permissions screen. tools/setup-phone.sh clears all three over adb.

How you knowtools/setup-phone.sh on a phone that has never seen the app gets to a working run without a trip into Settings that the script did not tell you about.
8

Release plumbing

Its own signing key, the dist task producing a versioned APK plus a stable filename plus update.json, the tag-triggered workflow, and the self-update check pointed at this repo's latest release. All four are lifted from fassistant-android with the names changed.

How you knowgit tag v0.1.0 produces a release carrying an APK and a manifest, apksigner verify --print-certs shows the new key, and an installed 0.1.0 offers 0.2.0 when it appears.

Open questions

1. Does a run need to survive the screen going off?

A long unattended run is the obvious use, and it is also the case where Doze and the screen state get in the way. The sibling repo has a whole architecture for staying alive through this; copying it here would be a lot of machinery.

Recommend: no. A gesture dispatched at a dark, locked screen does not do anything useful, so the honest behaviour is a foreground service that keeps the run alive while the screen is on and a wake lock held only for the length of a run. If long unattended runs turn out to matter, that is a later milestone with its own probe, not something to build on spec.

2. New signing key, or share fassistant's?

Nothing forces them apart — the app ids differ, so one key could sign both. The existing key sits in ~/Sites/.fassistant-signing/fassistant.jks.

Recommend: a new key, ~/Sites/.fassistant-signing/fassistant-click.jks, same RSA 4096 with a generated password. Installed copies use the signature to decide an update is genuine, so one key signing two apps means one compromised key costs both. The sibling's changelog already records what replacing a key costs — a forced uninstall — which is a good reason to get it right on the first release.

3. How much does the panel need to do on its own?

The original puts nearly everything on the floating panel, so you rarely open the app proper. That is more overlay UI, and overlay UI is the fiddliest thing here — it has to stay usable while sitting on top of the app you care about.

Recommend: panel does start, stop, pause, add point, and pick a saved script. Editing timings, naming and managing scripts, and settings live in the real activity. Move things onto the panel later if reaching for the app turns out to be annoying in practice.

4. Which phones are the target?

Milestone 0's answers differ by Android version, and gate 3 only bites on 13 and later. The sibling was developed against a OnePlus 5T on Android 10.

Recommend: the OnePlus 5T plus one phone on Android 13 or later. Two phones on opposite sides of the restricted-settings change is what makes the probe worth running; one phone on either side leaves half the answers unknown.

Assumptions

Decided rather than asked. Any of these is cheap to overturn now and expensive later.

  • Nothing comes out of the original APK. The feature list is the only thing taken from Click Assistant — no decompiled code, no extracted assets, no icon, no name, no screen layouts. A functional equivalent written from scratch is ordinary work; lifting the implementation or the branding is not, and there is no reason to do it when the whole app is one platform API.
  • Sideloaded onto your own phones, with adb available. This shapes the permission story completely. A Play Store build would need a different answer to gate 3 and a policy justification for the accessibility grant.
  • Two independent APKs on the phone. Its own repo, app id, key and release, per the layout decision. No shared code with fassistant-android — the conventions are copied, the source is not, because a shared library between two repos is a third thing to maintain for two files' worth of reuse.
  • English only, all user-facing text in strings.xml, same as the sibling.
  • No analytics, no network beyond the single self-update URL. The INTERNET permission exists for that and nothing else.
